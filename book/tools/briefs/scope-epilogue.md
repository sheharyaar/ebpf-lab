The argument. The book followed one program, the write counter, through every stage of its life, measured the cost of each stage, and then asked whether the verifier at the centre of it can be trusted. The epilogue looks back over the whole tower once, names the traps the reader is most likely to meet in practice, sets out the argument the research community and the maintainers are having about the verifier as a design, and says where eBPF runs beyond Linux and where a reader goes next. It does not teach anything new in depth: it gathers, weighs and points.

Read first: every chapter's "What it costs" box and "When it goes wrong" table (src/machine.html through src/capstone.html, in ORDER; numbers.py's ORDER and tools/order.py show give the order), the prologue src/ch00.html (the epilogue answers its opening question), and Part V (src/ch17.html, src/proof.html, src/agni.html, src/prevail.html). What it covers:
- The whole tower: one figure that shows the counter's path from C source to a count read in user space, every stage labelled with the chapter that covered it and the cost the book measured there (from the costs boxes, with their provenance); one table that gathers the book's decision rules in a line each. This is the book's summary in one spread.
- Traps: the failures that bit the counter, gathered from the chapters' "When it goes wrong" tables into one table of perhaps a dozen rows, chosen for how often a working engineer meets them (for example the loader's prev_key loop, lost updates on a shared key, runs skipped as recursion misses at a preemptible hook, a full hash that refuses new keys silently, per-CPU values charged to no cgroup, a kfunc gone in the next kernel, an LRU hash that evicts the heaviest keys): symptom, cause, the chapter that explains it (xref). No new claims: every row must already be in a chapter.
- Limits, and the argument about the verifier: what the verifier cannot prove or has got wrong (the SoK on eBPF memory safety, Huang, Payer, Qian, Sampson, Tan, Jaeger, IEEE S&P 2025: re-check its counts of bugs and of unprovable memory operations); "Kernel extension verification is untenable" (Jia, Sahu, Oswald, Williams, Le, Xu, HotOS 2023); KFlex (Dwivedi, Iyer, Kashyap, SOSP 2024); Rex (Jia et al., USENIX ATC 2025). State each position fairly in a sentence or two with one quotation each, then what the kernel itself has done since (from the book's chapters: kfuncs over frozen helpers, arenas, bounded loops and may_goto, private stacks, tokens, program signing; verify the release of each you name, on git.kernel.org through the ctx fetch tool or the GitHub API, and do not add mechanisms the book does not cover). Then the systems engineer's position: what this means for deciding whether to run eBPF code in production, in two or three sentences, without taking a side the evidence does not support.
- Where it went: eBPF beyond Linux in one paragraph each, from their current documentation: the ISA standard (RFC 9669), eBPF for Windows (which verifies with PREVAIL, chapter 17), uBPF and other user-space runtimes, and hardware: the learner is interested in eBPF on FPGAs, so give hXDP (OSDI 2020) and eHDL (ASPLOS 2023 or wherever it was published: check) one paragraph on what they had to build to run XDP programs in hardware, and why maps, helpers and instruction-level parallelism are the hard parts (from the papers' abstracts and introductions, fetched).
- Where to go next: a short reading list in the book's read-with form (what to read first, what to skip, how long), five or six items at most, chosen to continue each part of the book (for example the kernel's BPF documentation index, the verifier's own docs, one paper per research line above, the hXDP paper), each fetched and checked.
