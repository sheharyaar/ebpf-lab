Reading Agni: proving Linux's range analysis

Agni compiles the verifier's own C into logic, checks each arithmetic and branch operator of the range analysis against a claim of soundness on sixteen kernels, and writes a short program for most of the bugs it finds. We read how it does that, re-derive one of its verdicts by hand, and mark what its proofs leave out, v7.2 included.

Sixteen kernels, two verdicts each

The paper is Verifying the Verifier: eBPF Range Analysis Verification by Harishankar Vishwanathan, Matan Shachnai, Srinivas Narayana and Santosh Nagarakatte, presented at CAV 2023. We read the authors' copy of 30 May 2023 and the repository of its tool, Agni, on GitHub. It checks the part of the verifier that followed through the log: the tnum and the ranges of every scalar register, and the code that updates them. Its scope is 36 instructions, the operations add, sub, and, or, xor and the three shifts in 64-bit and 32-bit form, and ten conditional jumps in both forms.

gives the verdicts for seven of the sixteen kernels it checked, from 4.14 to 5.19. Each kernel was checked twice, against a general specification, gen, and a refined one, sro, whose difference is the subject of . Under gen no kernel passed, 5.19 included, while under sro every kernel from 5.13 on passed and every older one failed. The paper reads the second result as a proof that recent kernels are sound with respect to value tracking.

A violation in the table is one operator whose output misses a value the program can really produce, counted once for each of the five descriptions it misses. For each violation left under sro, Agni searched for an eBPF program that reaches it and found one for about 97 percent, never longer than three instructions. Among them the authors count 27 bugs that nobody had reported, all fixed by then in patches written for other reasons. On their test machine, 90 percent of the operators were checked within about 100 minutes, and the slowest checks ran for 2,000 minutes or more.

The artifact's README at tag v1.0 walks through one such search on Linux 5.9, and quotes it. For the 64-bit OR the refined check fails in the two 32-bit descriptions, and the search names a skeleton of two instructions, a signed comparison (JSLE) followed by the OR. Its authors built a whole program around that skeleton and ran it in a virtual machine with 5.9. After the OR, R1's tnum knows all 32 low bits to be 1, so the low half is 0xffffffff, or −1 read as signed. The 32-bit bounds say [−1, −2], a range with no value in it.

The log fits the early return of . Both low halves are known, so scalar32_min_max_or() keeps the destination's old 32-bit bounds and the tidy-up then narrows them past each other, until commit 049c4e13714e removed the return in 5.13-rc4 as the fix for CVE-2021-3490. What differs is the route. The previous chapter reached the bug by writing one function as a formula and checking it, whereas Agni reached it, with a program, from the compiled C of every operator, run kernel by kernel.

Five descriptions, one claim

The refresher of wrote soundness for one abstract domain: for every x in γ(A) and every y in γ(B), the result of the real operation lies in γ of the abstract one. proved that claim for tnum_and() alone. The verifier, however, keeps five abstract values for every scalar, as showed. They are the tnum and four ranges, unsigned and signed, for all 64 bits and for the low 32, which Agni names u64, s64, u32 and s32; kernels before 5.7, which added the 32-bit ranges, have only three.

Taken together the five form a product domain, a domain whose values are tuples with one value from each of several domains and whose γ is the intersection of what the parts allow (§2 of the paper). A register P stands for γt(Pt) ∩ γu64(Pu64) ∩ … ∩ γs32(Ps32), so a number belongs to P only if all five descriptions allow it. In the textbook form of such a domain each component has operators of its own, and separate reduction functions narrow one component with another, so that each piece can be proved sound alone.

The kernel does not divide its code that way, and follows one 64-bit AND to show where. tnum_and() works on tnums alone, but scalar32_min_max_and() and scalar_min_max_and() take the unsigned minimum from the tnum just computed and copy the signed range from the unsigned one. Code that reads the output of one domain while it computes another is neither an operator of one domain nor a reduction, which takes finished values (Figure 2(b) of the paper). With no pieces to prove one at a time, Agni proves each instruction's code in a single query, which the paper calls one-shot reasoning.

The claim, the paper's Equation 2, lets x range over the numbers that all five descriptions of P allow and y over those of Q, and asks it for each description D of the output in turn.

∀P, Q, ∀x ∈ γ(P), ∀y ∈ γ(Q) :  x & y ∈ γD(AND(P, Q)D).

Here AND is the kernel's whole operator, including the tail of three calls that every ALU operator ends with, and sets the claim beside the one of and the variants that follow. A conditional jump writes no register, yet the verifier learns something on each side of it, since after if r1 < r2 the taken branch may lower r1's maximum. Its code therefore returns both registers for both outcomes, twenty abstract values, and the claim for a jump (Equation 3) is that the real x and y lie in the refined values of the branch they take.

The shared tail, and bugs no program reaches

Under Equation 2 every kernel failed, and in 5.19 the six bitwise operators, and, or and xor in both widths, still failed. The precondition was too generous. It admits any five descriptions that share a number, including combinations that no program produces, such as a signed range of [0, 10] beside an unsigned range that reaches past 263.

Such combinations never reach an operator because of the tail. Every ALU operator ends with the same three calls, __update_reg_bounds(), __reg_deduce_bounds() and __reg_bound_offset(), which 5.19 gathered into reg_bounds_sync(), and the paper calls them the shared suffix. Agni treats the suffix as a shared refinement operator, sro for short, a function that narrows each description of a register by the others and runs on every register an operator returns. A program starts with registers that hold constants or unknown values, which the tail leaves unchanged (the authors checked this with the solver), so every register an operator receives has passed through it.

The refined specification, Equation 4, therefore passes the inputs through the tail first, so that x must lie in γ(sro(P)) and y in γ(sro(Q)). It removed every violation from 5.13 to 5.19 and about 6.8 percent of them in older kernels. What gen reports and sro does not is latent unsoundness, a wrong output that an operator gives for inputs no program can feed it, because those inputs never come out of the tail.

Latent did not stay acceptable for long. In AND, OR and XOR the signed range was copied from the unsigned one whenever neither input's signed minimum was negative, which gives a minimum above the maximum when the unsigned range crosses 263. Harishankar Vishwanathan's commit 1f586614f3ff, "bpf: Harden and/or/xor value tracking in verifier", released in 6.10, copies the range only when it stays on one side of the sign boundary. Its message says that earlier work, the CAV paper among it, had reported the case, and that Agni counted it as a false positive because its inputs pass through the tail.

The patch had a second purpose, which shows from the follow-up paper of SAS 2024. As the tail grew, a formula holding operator and tail together took 36 hours for 5.19 and 6.3, and from 6.4 on the solver ran for weeks and timed out without an answer. Once every operator is sound alone, the operator and the tail can be proved apart, the tail with the identity as its real operation, and the two results compose. Each kernel then took under 30 minutes, and the artifact's --modular option, which works this way, refuses AND, OR and XOR on kernels before 6.10.

From verifier.c to a formula

Writing each operator out by hand, as did for one, would have to be repeated for every kernel, and the paper calls it tedious and error-prone. Agni compiles the verifier instead, along the path of . A small wrapper for each instruction marks two registers as scalars and calls adjust_scalar_min_max_vals() with a fixed opcode, and clang turns verifier.c, the wrappers and tnum.c into LLVM IR.

Custom passes then remove every function that writes no register state and inline every call that remains, and LLVM's constant propagation and dead-code elimination delete every case of the opcode switch but one. Inlining everything works because no function on these paths recurses or loops, so each instruction becomes a single function whose control flow is acyclic, and the tool stops with an error when that is not so. A pass taken from the SeaHorn verifier turns each memcpy into plain stores.

The LLVMToSMT pass writes the result in SMT-LIB. Each IR value becomes a bit-vector, each basic block is guarded by the conditions of the paths into it, and a phi node selects its input by the condition of the edge it arrived on. Memory needs no byte array, because these functions reach it only through fields of struct bpf_reg_state. The pass keeps a tree of bit-vectors for each of the two registers and makes a new tree at each store, following the versions of memory that LLVM's MemorySSA analysis computes.

shows the result for the 64-bit AND of 5.10 as the repository keeps it, 1,839 lines that declare 548 bit-vectors and make a single assertion. Not all of the path is automatic. The jumps go through wrappers that copy part of check_cond_jmp_op() by hand, in five versions for ranges of kernels from 5.3-rc1 to 6.10-rc5. The tail has wrappers of its own, and when the kernel changes those functions a person writes another copy.

From a failed check to a program

A violation that survives Equation 4 may still be latent, because the tail removes only some of the impossible inputs. Agni settles the question by building a program that reaches the violation, a search the paper calls differential synthesis, finding a program whose real run and whose abstract interpretation by the verifier disagree.

A register's five descriptions are initial when they describe a constant or an entirely unknown value, the only two kinds of register a program starts with. For one instruction, Equation 5 asks Z3 for two initial registers, and values in them, that put the result outside its descriptions. The question has a single ∃ and is easier than the ∀∃ of most synthesis problems, since one input and one program suffice.

For a program of k instructions, Agni enumerates the opcodes of the first k − 1, which form the skeleton. The solver then draws every operand from an initial register or an earlier result, each of which must hold its real value (Equation 6). A branch becomes a choice whose two targets both lead to the next slot, so that its refined registers can feed later instructions (Equation 7).

The search tries one instruction, then two, then three, and at four it timed out. For 5.5 no violation could be shown by a single instruction, and for one known bug Agni produced a program of two instructions where the published proof of concept used four. The authors compiled and booted each kernel in QEMU, ran the programs, and found the verifier's log and the real values in agreement with the solver's model in every case they checked. A violation with no program stays open, since a program of four or more instructions may reach it, or it may be latent after all.

One verdict, re-derived by hand

To see what such a verdict asserts, we re-derived Agni's verdicts for the 64-bit AND with the method of , as a formula written by hand. book/labs/agni/repro.py encodes the taint check at the top of adjust_scalar_min_max_vals(), then tnum_and(), scalar32_min_max_and(), scalar_min_max_and() and the tail, from verifier.c of v5.12 and v5.13 and tnum.c of v5.13, with the five membership tests of Agni's checker. It takes 97 lines of Python for about 260 lines of C. Diffing those functions between the two versions came first, and only the early return of differs.

A hand encoding is what Agni exists to avoid, and writing this one shows the cost. Each comparison had to be read for its signedness again, and the tail had to be copied in its order for that kernel. In 5.12 the tail ends by narrowing the tnum and does not narrow the bounds again, while v7.2's tail does. The v7.2 order copied into a 5.12 encoding would describe a kernel that never existed, and nothing in the solver's answer would say so.

gives the verdicts, which agree with Agni's. Under sro, 5.12 fails in u32 and s32 and holds in u64, s64 and the tnum, the pattern the artifact's README reports for AND on 5.9, and 5.13 holds in all five. Under gen, 5.13 fails in s64 and s32, the latent case that commit 1f586614f3ff removed, with signed minima of zero or more beside an unsigned maximum above 263. Modular mode, the operator without its tail, fails for 6.9 and holds for 6.10, which is SAS 2024's before and after for that commit.

The counterexample for 5.12, asked for small numbers with --small, is the early return once more. Both low halves are known after tnum_and(), so 5.12 keeps the destination's 32-bit bounds of [6, 6]. The __update_reg_bounds() call at the end of scalar_min_max_and() then lowers the maximum to 4, the value the new tnum allows, and leaves u32 at [6, 4], while the real low half of x & y is 4.

With --synth the script runs Equation 6 with AND as the only instruction, and shows the result. One instruction cannot reach the bug, since an unknown register has an unknown low half and a constant is known in all 64 bits. Two can, because an unknown value ANDed with 0x200000000 is known to be 0 in its low half with one unknown bit above it, and a constant ANDed with that register takes the early return. For 5.13 both searches answer unsat.

The hand encoding is small and fast. The five sro queries for 5.12 take about one second, against 31 seconds for AND on 5.9 in the artifact's README on its authors' machine. The query is 64,414 bytes of SMT-LIB, against 100,001 bytes for Agni's AND on 5.10 plus 48,684 for its tail. The difference comes from choices a person made, a few variables per register and shared subterms, where Agni keeps one variable per IR value. The work moved from the solver to whoever reads the C, and that reader is the part Agni removes.

What a proof by Agni leaves out

The paper's §7 lists four caveats. Only range analysis is covered, so register liveness and the handling of speculative execution are outside. Multiplication is left out because its formula times out at 64 bits, and it was checked with 8-bit vectors only. The trusted computing base, the software that must be correct for a proof to hold, includes LLVM, Z3 and the translation passes, which the authors unit-tested, and the synthesis is incomplete.

The repository narrows the scope further, as lists. Division, modulo, negation and the unconditional jump are marked to be skipped, and every wrapper sets both registers to SCALAR_VALUE, so pointer arithmetic, which adjust_ptr_min_max_vals() handles, never enters a formula. Nor do the walk and its pruning, precision tracking, the JIT or the checks on helper and kfunc arguments. Agni proves that the verifier's descriptions of numbers are sound for the instructions it encodes, in the kernels it has checked, if its translation and its glue are right; how the verifier then uses those descriptions lies outside the proof.

One gap appeared after publication. A JNE whose two registers had disjoint ranges produced impossible bounds on the branch that cannot run. Agni's claim for jumps looks only at the branch the real values take, so it accepted them (issue 15 of the repository, November 2023). Such a branch can still run speculatively, which is why the verifier checks it, and the --weak_spec option added in July 2024 also requires well-formed outputs on the other side.

Linux v7.2 differs from every kernel Agni has checked. Eduard Zingerman's commit 256f0071f9b6 of April 2026, first released in v7.2, introduced the circular ranges of . A register now holds a tnum and two arcs, r64 and r32, in place of four pairs of bounds (). Agni's membership tests are written for five descriptions and would need rewriting before it could check v7.2. The commit says that the arc operations were verified with the CBMC model checker, with the tests kept in a separate repository.

Agni's own daily run, over eight trees, passed for the stable kernels 5.4.y to 6.12.y until its last run on 14 May 2026. On bpf-next its jump jobs began to fail on 4 April 2026. By 14 May every job for that tree failed, those we inspected before any formula was written, and GitHub then switched the schedule off for inactivity. The public record holds no Agni verdict for v7.2.

The kernel meanwhile came to rely on the result. Commit b254c6d816e5, in v7.1, refines both sides of every branch and treats an ill-formed result as proof that a side is dead, "because the branch operators are sound (verified with Agni)". v7.2 keeps that reasoning in the comment above simulate_both_branches_taken() at line 15187 of verifier.c. On the public record, its pruning of dead branches therefore rests on Agni's checks of the earlier ranges and on CBMC's checks of the arc operations.

What it costs

CPU
Agni on two 10-core Skylake processors at 2.2 GHz: 90 percent of operators in about 100 minutes (CAV 2023); a whole kernel 2.5 hours for 4.14, 36 hours for 5.19, and no answer after weeks from 6.4 on, with operator and tail in one formula; and under 5, 15 or 30 minutes with them apart (SAS 2024). The README's run on 5.9: AND in 9.5 s (gen) and 31 s (sro), JEQ in 3,911 s (sro). Our reproduction, on an AMD Ryzen 7 8845HS with Python 3.14.10 and z3-solver 5.4.1, 13 November 2026, medians of five runs: five sro queries 1.0 s for 5.12 and 0.88 s for 5.13, gen 0.30 s, modular 0.07 to 0.10 s; the two-instruction search 2.3 s for 5.12 and 3.7 s to answer unsat for 5.13.
Memory
The reproduction's Python process peaked at 92 to 100 MB. The paper names its machine's 192 GB and gives no peak for Agni.
Size
Agni is about 5,000 lines (LSFMM+BPF 2025 slides). Its formulas for 5.10: AND 100,001 bytes, the tail 48,684, JLT 738,840; for 6.12 the tail is 180,891 bytes and JLT 2,160,687. Ours: 97 lines of Python for about 260 lines of C, a 64,414-byte query, 392 lines in the script.
Upkeep
Five copies of the jump code and three of the tail, kept by hand; a daily run of 184 jobs over eight trees, off since 14 May 2026.

Before posting a change to a range operator or to the tail, run Agni in modular mode on the patched tree for the operators the change touches and for BPF_SYNC, which takes minutes, and run the selftests; a change to how ranges are stored or how branches refine needs new glue or a proof of its own, as the arcs got from CBMC, and the cover letter should say which. From "Agni found no bug in X", conclude only that the encoded ALU and jump operators of X are sound at 64 bits under its specification; it says nothing about a kernel it was not run on, nor about pointers, pruning or multiplication.

When it goes wrong

The first two rows of are what a hand reproduction most often gets wrong: an encoding that drops the tail, from the inputs or from the operator, reports bugs the kernel does not have. Such a report can at least be checked, by asking the refined question or by searching for a program. The next four rows are the opposite danger, a verdict read for more than it covers. No command run on the verdict will reveal that, and only a look at the specification, the kernel version and the list of encoded instructions settles it.

Hint 1 · direction

The output's u32 range has its minimum above its maximum. Of the two ways through scalar32_min_max_and(), one leaves the 32-bit bounds untouched.

Hint 2 · mechanism

A low half is known when the low 32 bits of the mask are 0, as in src (0x5; 0x100000000) and out (0x4; 0x100000000). In v5.12, scalar32_min_max_and() begins at line 6532 and scalar_min_max_and() ends with a call at line 6600.

Hint 3 · nearly there

In the gen run, compare dst's s64 range with its u64 maximum. With a signed minimum of zero or more, __reg64_deduce_bounds() in the tail would cut the unsigned maximum to the signed one.

Recap

Agni compiles the verifier's C for each of 36 ALU and jump instructions into one SMT formula and checks it against a claim of soundness over the product of the five descriptions, one query per description. Under the general claim every kernel from 4.14 to 5.19 failed; with the inputs passed through the tail that every operator ends with, the refined claim proved 5.13 to 5.19 sound and left the rest of the general claim's reports as latent unsoundness. For the violations that remain, differential synthesis found programs of at most three instructions for about 97 percent, and our hand encoding of the 64-bit AND re-derives the verdicts for 5.12, 5.13, 6.9 and 6.10 in about a second, at the price of reading every line of C. The proofs cover value tracking for the encoded instructions only, and no Agni run on v7.2, whose ranges are arcs, is public, although v7.1 and v7.2 rely on the branch operators being sound. The next chapter reads a verifier built the other way, from abstract interpretation outward, where Linux's grew by patches.

Twenty for a jump and five for an ALU instruction. A conditional jump refines both registers on both outcomes, and each register carries five descriptions, so 2 × 2 × 5 = 20, the count the paper gives under Equation 3; an ALU instruction writes one register, 1 × 5. Before 5.7 a register had three descriptions, since the 32-bit ranges came with commit 3f50f132d840, so the counts were 12 and 3, and Agni's checker changes its claims at 5.7-rc1. It is tempting to count the source register of an ALU instruction as an output too, but the kernel leaves it unchanged and Agni checks only the destination.

Not on that evidence. Inputs whose signed minima are 0 or more while their unsigned maxima pass 263 never come out of the tail, whose __reg64_deduce_bounds() cuts the unsigned maximum to the signed one, so the counterexample shows latent unsoundness. The test is the refined claim: pass both inputs through the tail and ask again, as repro.py does by default for AND, and Agni proved 5.14 sound that way (). A search for a program should then come back empty. The case was still worth a patch, which 1f586614f3ff made in 6.10, but reporting it as an exploitable bug of 5.14 would be wrong.

Nothing about the multiplication or the access. Agni left multiplication out because its formula times out at 64 bits, checked it with 8-bit vectors only, and the repository marks it to be skipped. The offset into the map value is pointer arithmetic, which adjust_ptr_min_max_vals() handles, and every wrapper makes both registers scalars, so that code never enters a formula; nor does the access check of . A passing run supports only that the encoded ALU and jump operators of the 6.6.y head it built were sound at 64 bits. Reading a green run as "the verifier is verified" claims far more than it shows.

Because the kernel's code reads across domains while it computes. scalar_min_max_and() takes the unsigned minimum from the tnum just computed and copies the signed range from the unsigned one, so it is neither an operator of one domain, which reads only that domain's inputs, nor a reduction, which takes finished outputs (Figure 2(b) of the paper). Agni therefore proved each instruction in one query. The 2024 split is of another kind, operator from tail rather than domain from domain. It became possible when commit 1f586614f3ff made AND, OR and XOR sound without the tail, since the soundness of the operator and of the tail then compose, which the --modular option relies on from 6.10.

That the public record holds no Agni verdict for v7.2. v7.2 keeps a tnum and two arcs per register (commit 256f0071f9b6), where Agni's checker expects four pairs of bounds, and Agni's daily run failed on bpf-next from April 2026 until GitHub switched it off on 14 May. The evidence that exists is narrower: CBMC checks of the arc operations by their author, Agni's checks of the branch operators cited by commit b254c6d816e5 for the earlier ranges, an SMT proof of tnum_step() (commit 76e954155b45), and the selftests. Even a full Agni pass would cover value tracking only. Quoting "proved sound since 5.13" for v7.2 stretches a result about other code.

Because a real program can start only from registers that hold constants or unknown values, and init() says that each operand's five descriptions are of one of those two kinds. Without it the solver could pick any input descriptions, including ones only the general claim admits, and the program would show a latent case that no run can reach. A model gives the concrete operands and their descriptions, so the instruction, with its operands loaded as constants or read from unknown data, is a program that runs and shows the mismatch. An unsat answer proves nothing about the bug; it only rules out programs of one instruction, which is why Equation 6 adds earlier ones.

Nothing on the value-tracking paths recurses or loops, so inlining every call ends, and each instruction becomes one function whose control flow is acyclic, with a finite path condition for every block. Memory needs no byte array because the functions touch it only through the fields of struct bpf_reg_state in two registers, so Agni models two disjoint trees of bit-vectors, indexed as LLVM's getelementptr indexes fields and versioned by MemorySSA. Tools such as SMACK and SeaHorn model memory as a linear array of bytes, which §8 calls a poor fit for kernel code. A common misreading is that loops are unrolled; there are none to unroll, and the current tool stops if one appears.

The first says that every bug in 5.5 needs an input description that no initial register has, such as a tnum known in some bits only or a range a branch has narrowed, so an earlier instruction must build it. The second has two readings, which §7 gives: a program of four or more instructions may reach the violation, which the enumeration timed out before trying, or the violation is latent and reported because the refined claim still admits impossible inputs. Figure 5(b) leaves 8 of 30 such cases for 5.5 and 3 of 21 for 4.14. Calling them false alarms of Agni's goes further than the paper, which leaves them open.

What this shows. In 5.12 both low halves are known after tnum_and(), since the masks of src and out are 0 in their low 32 bits, so the early return at lines 6544 and 6545 keeps the destination's u32 of [6, 6]. The __update_reg_bounds() at line 6600, at the end of scalar_min_max_and(), lowers the maximum to the tnum's 4 and gives [6, 4], while the real low half is 4; in 5.13 the same case is marked known as [4, 4]. The gen counterexample's dst has a signed range wholly below 263 beside an unsigned maximum of 0x8af9cb4b301068d1, above it. The tail would have cut that maximum to the signed one, so the verifier never holds such a register, and --synth finds no program for 5.13.

Common wrong turn. Blaming tnum_and(): the output's tnum, (0x4; 0x100000000), holds the real result, as the tnum column of the verdict line confirms. Another is to read the gen counterexample as a bug of 5.13 and stop there.
