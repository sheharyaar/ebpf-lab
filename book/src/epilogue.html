The counter's life on one page

The write counter is the program of , 27 slots of bytecode on the sys_enter_write tracepoint. It keeps one count per thread in a hash map of 1,024 entries, which its loader reads. Each chapter from on took it through one more stage and measured what that stage cost. stacks those measurements in the order the counter meets them, from clang's output to the last grace period after the loader exits.

The rows fall into three prices by when they are paid. Every write pays for the run, 144 to 162 ns, which adds 230 to 245 ns to the write() around it. At a million writes a second that is about a quarter of one CPU. A load pays once for the walk, the JIT and the attach, from about a hundred microseconds to a few milliseconds, and a detach waits 29 to 46 ms for a grace period. The last three rows fall on whoever changes the verifier and never on the program.

gathers the decision rule that closed each costs box, one line a chapter. Most of the rules turn on a number the running kernel reports. The run's cost is run_time_ns over run_cnt once kernel.bpf_stats_enabled is 1, and the walk's size is the processed count in the verifier's statistics line. Skipped runs show as recursion_misses in bpftool prog show, and memory as memlock beside the cgroup's memory.current. Each was read in a lab of this book with one command.

Thirteen traps

The chapters' "When it goes wrong" tables hold 172 rows. Most are messages from the verifier or libbpf that name their own cause once the chapter has been read. keeps thirteen of the other kind, chosen for how often an engineer with a working program meets them: on a busier machine, on the next kernel, or after the loader exits. Eleven of the thirteen print no error at all, so the first sign is a wrong total or a loader that never stops.

Three rows share one symptom, a total short of the writes, and two counters tell them apart. If run_cnt matches the writes, every run happened, and CPUs overwrote each other's updates to a shared key. If recursion_misses grows, runs were skipped at a preemptible hook before the map was reached. If new threads are missing while old ones count on, the hash is full and refuses new keys. The fixes differ as much as the causes, an atomic add or a per-CPU map for the first, a hook where preemption is off for the second, and an LRU hash or a sketch for the third.

What the verifier cannot promise

Part V held the verifier to the standard of a proof, and every result came with a boundary. proved tnum_add() at every width and watched Z3 find CVE-2021-3490 in the 32-bit bounds Linux shipped from 5.7 until 2021. showed that Agni's proofs cover the range operators it encodes, on the kernels it ran on, and leave pruning, pointers and multiplication outside. found a soundness theorem that holds for a model of eBPF, and a 2026 advisory that marked the gap between that model and PREVAIL's code. Four recent papers argue about what such boundaries mean for the verifier as a design.

The SoK on eBPF memory safety by Huang, Payer, Qian, Sampson, Tan and Jaeger (IEEE S&P 2025) gathers the evidence. It counts 325 bugs caused by memory errors that Syzbot has reported in the eBPF subsystem, 169 of them since 2023 and 45 still unfixed. It sorts the verifier's failures into checks that "are unsound, incomplete, and have a limited scope that prevents comprehensive validation of memory safety". Its measurement of real programs is milder: only 1.62 to 3.74 per cent of the memory operations in public eBPF programs, 37 to 85 a program on average, could not be shown safe by any existing defence. The authors take that share as grounds for enforcing full memory safety in ways that keep today's programs fast and loadable.

Jia, Sahu, Oswald, Williams, Le and Xu went further in "Kernel extension verification is untenable" (HotOS 2023). They "argue that eBPF's verifier—the source of its safety guarantees—has become a liability", first because static checking of bytecode has soundness and completeness problems that grow with the verifier. Their second reason is that each limit the verifier imposes gets worked around with a new helper, a way into the kernel that the verifier never checks. They counted 249 helpers in Linux 5.18 and at least 18 security bugs fixed in helpers over two years. In place of the verifier they sketch extensions in safe Rust, signed by a trusted toolchain and checked at load, with light run-time mechanisms to make programs end.

KFlex, by Dwivedi, Iyer and Kashyap (SOSP 2024), keeps the verifier and narrows its job. It "separates the safety of kernel-owned resources (e.g., kernel memory) from the safety of extension-specific resources (e.g., extension memory)". The first is proved statically, and the second is guarded by cheap run-time checks on an extension heap and by a mechanism that cancels an extension that runs too long. The paper reports several of its mechanisms upstreamed. It describes the arena of , which Alexei Starovoitov contributed in parallel, as reaching feature parity with its heaps at a limit of 4 GB.

Rex, by Jia, Qin, Craun, Lukiyanov, Bansal, Phan, Le, Franke, Jamjoom, Xu and Williams (USENIX ATC 2025), builds safe Rust extensions on Linux 6.11. It calls the problem the "language-verifier gap". The evidence is 72 commits to Cilium, Aya and Katran in which programs that were already safe had to be reshaped until the verifier accepted them, the work that and did by hand. Rex extensions call the kernel through a crate of safe wrappers, with a small run-time for exceptions, stack safety and termination and no separate static verification. The price, which the paper states, is a Rust toolchain inside the trusted computing base.

What Linux changed instead

Linux kept its verifier and changed what surrounds it. lists the changes this book covered, each with the first release whose tag contains its commit. Several move the way the papers asked. may_goto puts a run-time bound on a loop, which on x86-64 in v7.2 cuts the loop off after a quarter of a second, and the arena gives a program memory whose bounds and locks it manages itself, as KFlex's heaps did. Signed programs, from 6.18, let a loader prove where a program came from, the role the HotOS design gave to its toolchain's signature.

None of these removes the walk. A signature is checked inside BPF_PROG_LOAD before bpf_check() runs, and the walk still follows every path of the signed program afterwards. The helper list stops at 211, and the comment that closes it in include/uapi/linux/bpf.h sends new work to kfuncs. A kfunc's body is ordinary kernel C that the verifier never reads, so the way around the verifier that the HotOS paper warned about has moved from helpers to kfuncs, checked at the call by BTF type and flag. Tokens and private stacks answer narrower complaints, who may load in a container and how much stack a tracing program's nested calls may use, and neither changes what the walk proves.

Running it in production

For an engineer deciding whether to run eBPF code in production, the evidence points at the loader as much as at the program. A program the verifier accepts is safe under the verifier's model of that kernel, a model that Part V could prove only in parts, inside a subsystem where Syzbot has found 325 memory-error bugs. The risk therefore sits in two decisions, who may load programs and which kernels a program is tested on: the first is set by kernel.unprivileged_bpf_disabled, the capabilities of , tokens and signed loaders, and the second matters because several traps in appear only on a newer kernel.

eBPF beyond Linux

The instruction set the counter is written in became a standard in October 2024 as RFC 9669, whose read-with card is in . It defines conformance groups, so that a runtime can say which instructions it supports. Every implementation must support base32, while base64, the atomic groups, the division and multiplication groups and the deprecated packet group are optional. Its security section leaves what a verifier must check to future IETF work, so two runtimes can agree on every instruction and still accept different programs.

eBPF for Windows runs the same bytecode with PREVAIL as its verifier, in the modes that described. In the preferred native mode, bpf2c verifies a program and turns it into C that is built into a driver. A JIT mode verifies and compiles in a user-mode service, and an interpreter is kept for debug builds. In user space, uBPF is an Apache-licensed library because the kernel's implementation is GPL and, in its README's words, "can't be used in many projects". It has an interpreter and a JIT for x86-64 and Arm64, and in place of a verifier it offers a safe profile that checks pointers at run time.

Two other runtimes show the range. rbpf is a virtual machine in Rust with an interpreter, an x86-64 JIT and a small verifier, which its authors call "nothing close to the one for Linux kernel" and which users may replace. bpftime runs uprobes, system-call hooks and XDP programs in user space, compiled with LLVM or uBPF and checked by PREVAIL or by the kernel's own verifier. sets these beside Linux and the two hardware designs of the next section.

eBPF on an FPGA

A field-programmable gate array, or FPGA, is a chip of logic blocks and memories whose connections are set by loading a hardware design. One chip can thus become many different circuits. Network cards built around an FPGA can process a packet before it crosses PCIe to the host, and two research groups have run XDP programs on such cards. draws their two designs side by side.

hXDP, by Brunella and colleagues (OSDI 2020), starts from the finding that "the iterative execution model of eBPF is not a good fit for FPGA accelerators". Their clocks run five to ten times slower than a server CPU's. A one-instruction-a-cycle executor at 156 MHz would reach at most 2.8 million packets a second on a simple firewall that one 3.7 GHz core runs at 7.4 million. To close that gap the authors built a compiler, a core, and hardware for XDP's maps and helpers as the kernel defines them. The compiler removes instructions the hardware makes unnecessary, among them the bounds checks the verifier demands, and packs independent instructions together.

The core is a VLIW processor, short for very long instruction word, which issues several instructions in one cycle because the compiler grouped them ahead of time. Theirs, Sephirot, has four lanes, and on a NetFPGA board at 156.25 MHz the design used about 15 per cent of the FPGA to run the firewall at 6.53 million packets a second. Parallelism, helpers and maps were the hard parts. The core cannot reorder instructions at run time, so the compiler must find all the parallelism, and its single helper unit takes one call a cycle. Every map lives in one FPGA memory area, which a configurator shapes from the program's maps section.

eHDL, by Rivitti, Bifulco, Tulumello, Bonola and Pontarelli (ASPLOS 2023), builds a circuit for each program instead. It is a tool for high-level synthesis, the generation of a hardware design from a program written in a software language. It turns an unmodified XDP program into a pipeline whose stages each run one group of independent instructions, so that the pipeline holds a packet in every stage. Bounded loops make this possible, since a loop the verifier can bound unrolls into stages, and each helper call becomes a hardware block of its own. Maps are the hard part again, because "since programs are unrolled as a pipeline in hardware, this effectively means that race conditions might arise" between stages that read and write one entry.

On a Xilinx Alveo U50, a 100 Gb/s FPGA card, its five test programs used 6.5 to 13.3 per cent of the FPGA. They forwarded 64-byte packets at line rate, 148 million a second, with about a microsecond of latency, 10 to 100 times the throughput of processor-based offloads such as hXDP. The price is flexibility, because hXDP loads a new program at run time while synthesising an eHDL pipeline usually takes a few hours. Its map hazards are the counter's lost updates of in another form, two writers to one key, here two pipeline stages where the counter had two CPUs.

The counter, thirty-three years on

The prologue began in 1993 with a filter that could read one packet and sixteen words of scratch memory. Its jumps all went forward, and Linux 2.1.75 checked it with three rules before attaching it. The write counter reads the arguments of a system call, calls four helpers and keeps a map that user space reads. It passes a walk of 36 instructions by a verifier whose main file alone, kernel/bpf/verifier.c, has 20,065 lines in v7.2. sets the two programs side by side.

The design between the two has held. In both, the kernel runs a small program for a limited machine at a hook on its own path, and checks the program before its first run so that a mistake costs wrong answers and leaves the kernel running. What grew is how much the check must know, from three rules that fit in a margin note to a walk of every path that tracks ranges and tnums for every register. That walk is where the SoK's bugs and the proofs of Part V both sit.

A reader who has followed the counter can follow a verifier log down to the source line that wrote it and choose a hook and a map from measured costs. The same reader can tell which proof covers which part of the verifier, and can add a data structure to the kernel twice, as did. The next program will meet the same stages in the same order, and the tables of this epilogue say what each stage costs and how it can fail.

  2. A program uses bpf_for(), an arena and the kfunc bpf_task_from_pid(), and it must load on every kernel from 5.15 to 7.2. Which of the three must it give up or guard on older kernels, and how?
  3. A security team asks whether a third-party program that the verifier accepted is safe to run as root on production hosts. What can you claim from Part V and this epilogue, what can you not, and which settings from earlier chapters narrow the risk?
  4. Predict the outcome: a workload runs 100,000 short-lived threads and four that write constantly, and an operator wants per-thread write counts. Compare the counter's hash, an LRU hash of the same size and the count-min sketch on memory, the cost of reading every count, and what each loses.
  5. hXDP runs unmodified XDP programs. Why could it not run the write counter, which is also eBPF bytecode for the same instruction set?

Recap

The write counter's life costs nanoseconds per event, from microseconds to milliseconds per load and attach, and tens of milliseconds per detach, and every chapter's costs box ended in a rule that a number from the running kernel can test. Most traps a working program meets print no error, and a total short of the writes has three causes that run_cnt and recursion_misses tell apart. The verifier's subsystem has had hundreds of memory bugs: KFlex would narrow its job, while the HotOS paper and Rex would replace it with a safe language and a small run-time. Linux kept the walk and added may_goto, arenas, kfuncs, tokens, private stacks and signing around it. Beyond Linux, RFC 9669 fixes the instructions, Windows verifies with PREVAIL, user-space runtimes range from no verifier to PREVAIL, and on FPGAs hXDP built a processor while eHDL builds a circuit for each program.

The counter's system-call tracepoint, a per-CPU hash keyed by cgroup ID, and batch reads. A tracepoint is the hook for a program that must outlive upgrades (); it costs 127 ns empty and slows every system call while attached, getppid() from 320 to 354 ns. Many CPUs write each cgroup's key, so a shared hash updated by lookup and store loses counts, 66 per cent with eight writers (); a per-CPU hash took 30 ns, at 209 bytes an entry on 16 CPUs that no cgroup is charged for. One batch reads the counts at 95 ns a key. Expect about the counter's 144 to 162 ns a run. It is tempting to take fentry for its 57 ns, but that holds only on kernels whose BTF has the function.

The loop and the arena need a fallback, and the kfunc needs a guard. Open-coded iterators arrived in 6.4 and bpf_loop() in 5.17 (), so on 5.15 the loop must be a plain one with a small constant bound that the walk follows round by round, a dozen rounds or fewer by that chapter's rule. The arena arrived in 6.9, and before it the data has to live in a map with a slot per key (). kfuncs exist from 5.13, but any one of them can be missing from a given kernel, so declare it __weak and call it only behind bpf_ksym_exists() (). A common mistake is to expect CO-RE to cover all three, when it fixes field offsets and cannot supply what a kernel lacks ().

That it is safe under the verifier's model of that kernel, and no more. Agni's proofs cover the range operators it encodes, on the kernels it ran on, and no public Agni run covers v7.2 (); PREVAIL's theorem covers a model (); and the SoK counts 325 memory-error bugs in the subsystem, 45 of them unfixed. The helpers and kfuncs the program calls are kernel C that the verifier never reads. What narrows the risk is control over loading: kernel.unprivileged_bpf_disabled at 2 (), CAP_PERFMON only for a loader trusted with kernel memory (), a token for a container, a signed loader on 6.18 or later, and bpf_jit_harden where untrusted users load (). Reading Agni's result as a proof that the verifier is sound is the usual overreach.

On the capstone's workload of 100,000 light threads and four heavy ones, the hash of 1,024 entries took 83,968 bytes from creation and refused 296,940 inserts, silently unless the program checks for -E2BIG, and reading it took 100,004 bpf() calls and 69.5 ms (). The LRU hash of the same size, 82,816 bytes, never refused a key, yet it kept 740 of the loader's threads and none of the four heavy ones, which had stopped writing and were evicted first. The count-min sketch held an estimate for every thread in 108,760 bytes, read with 1.5 ms of loads and no system call, each within its bound of 700 and the heavy ones within 0.1 per cent. It is tempting to expect an LRU hash to keep the busiest keys, but it keeps the most recently used.

Because a program is tied to its hook, its helpers and its maps as much as to its instructions. hXDP runs XDP programs on a network card, where the hook is the packet path and the hardware provides XDP's maps and helpers. The counter is a tracepoint program on sys_enter_write in the host kernel: its first call, bpf_get_current_pid_tgid(), asks which thread made the system call, which a card never sees, and its map is read by a loader on the host through bpf(). The instructions are the same, those RFC 9669 standardises, and Sephirot would decode them. Answering that an FPGA cannot run 64-bit eBPF instructions misses this, since Sephirot's four lanes execute eBPF instructions directly.
