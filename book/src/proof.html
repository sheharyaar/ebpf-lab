Every case, then a solver

We start where the refresher stopped, with its checker, book/labs/ch17-check.py, and the bitwise AND of two tnums, tnum_and() at line 92 of kernel/bpf/tnum.c. For every pair of tnums the checker lists the values each one stands for, ANDs every pair of those values, and asks whether each result lies inside the tnum that tnum_and() returned. It found no counterexample at 4, 5, 6, 7 or 8 bits. At every width it also found each answer to be the tightest tnum possible, which the line "wider than the best answer: 0" in reports.

The price is in the same figure. Each extra bit gives a tnum and one of its members four ways to continue: a known 0, a known 1, or an unknown bit that is 0 or 1. The cases for two inputs therefore grow sixteenfold, from 65,536 at four bits to 4,294,967,296 at eight. The time follows them from 0.02 s to 987 s, about 0.23 µs a case, and at 64 bits there are 1664 = 2256 cases, which at that pace would take about 1070 seconds.

The last two commands hand the same claim to Z3 at 64 bits. It answers unsat for tnum_and() in 6 ms and for tnum_add() in 0.77 s, and the query that the refresher printed without running, , answers unsat in 0.79 s when it is run as printed. Because the query asks for a counterexample, unsat is a proof at that width. The rest of the chapter shows why the paper proof and the solver agree, how such a query is written, and what the answer looks like when the code is wrong.

What a tnum stands for, bit by bit

stored a tnum as two 64-bit words, a value and a mask, and the refresher wrote γ for the set of numbers it stands for. A proof needs that set as a test on one number. The membership relation of a tnum (v, m) is the test x & ~m == v, which holds when x, with every unknown bit cleared, equals the value. It is Equation 9 of the tnum paper (CGO 2022).

applies it to 1x0x, whose members are 8, 9, 12 and 13. The kernel's own form of the test is tnum_in() at line 210 of tnum.c, which regsafe() calls during pruning to ask whether one tnum lies inside another. Given the constant tnum_const(x) as its second argument, it clears from x the bits unknown in P and compares the rest with P's value.

The paper adds a second test, Equation 10. A well-formed tnum is one whose value has no 1 in a bit the mask marks unknown, v & m == 0, and the kernel builds no other kind. The two tests are not independent, because a number that passes the membership test leaves a value with every masked bit already cleared, so v & m is 0. A pair that breaks the rule, such as value 0010 with mask 0010, therefore has no member at all, and a claim that already says x is in P gains nothing from saying that P is well-formed. The assumption earns its place when membership is written another way, as the first row of shows.

tnum_and() proved by hand

We prove tnum_and() rather than tnum_add() because every operation in it works on each bit separately, so the argument never has to follow a carry from one bit into the next. The paper's proof for tnum_add() needs four lemmas about the carries (§III-B, Lemmas 2 to 5) and an appendix for their proofs, while for tnum_and() and the other bitwise operators it relied on the solver at 64 bits (§III-A). The kernel's code at line 92 of tnum.c first computes α = P.v | P.m, the bits that may be 1 in a member of P, and β likewise for Q. It then returns R = (v, m) with v = P.v & Q.v and m = α & β & ~v.

The claim is the refresher's soundness condition for this operation. Take any width n, any well-formed n-bit tnums P and Q, any x in γ(P) and any y in γ(Q). Then x & y must be a member of R: (x & y) & ~m == v.

One piece of notation keeps the steps short. We write a ⊑ b, read "a lies under b", when every bit set in a is also set in b, that is, when a & ~b == 0. Two facts about the relation carry the proof. ANDing both sides with the same word keeps it, and a ⊑ b makes a & b equal to a.

sets out the six steps. The first two place each input between the tnum's known 1s and the bits that may be 1. Membership says that P.v is x with the unknown bits cleared, so P.v lies under x. Splitting x into its known part P.v and its unknown part x & P.m shows that x lies under α.

ANDing those relations gives steps 3 and 4, a floor and a ceiling for x & y. Step 5 is the claim itself. By De Morgan's law ~m is ~(α & β) | v, which splits (x & y) & ~m into two parts; the ceiling makes the first 0 and the floor makes the second v.

Step 6 adds that the result is well-formed, which matters because R becomes the input of the next instruction's operation. No step mentions the width, so the argument holds for every n at once, and no step uses the well-formedness of P or Q, which membership already implies. Read bit by bit, the code says that a bit of the result is known 1 when it is known 1 in both inputs. It is unknown when it may be 1 in both and is not known 1, and every other bit is known 0.

The right-hand column is the visible link between proof and checker: steps.py tries each step's claim in all 65,536 four-bit cases, and every true step holds in all of them. The false step in the last row, the rule of the refresher's broken tnum_and_both, fails in 27,120 cases.

Those counts reveal a shortcut, because 65,536 is 164 and 38,416 is 144, the one-bit counts raised to the fourth power. Every word in this claim is computed bit by bit from the same bit of the inputs, so checking the sixteen one-bit cases already proves it for every width. The same argument shows that tnum_and() is as tight as a tnum can be. tnum_add() has no such shortcut, because a carry ties each bit of the sum to all the bits below it.

The same claim as a query

A solver searches for values that make formulas true, so a claim that should hold for every input is handed over turned round, as the refresher explained. shows the shape, and is the whole script. The variables are six bit-vectors of the chosen width: the two halves of P and of Q, and the two concrete inputs. The function tnum_and() is the kernel's four lines with the C types removed, because Python's |, & and ~ build Z3 expressions when their operands are bit-vectors. Two s.add() lines state the assumptions, and the third states that z = x & y is not a member of R.

An answer from such a query holds for one width only, and checking a claim at a fixed width is what the tnum paper calls bounded verification. Width is also what the solver pays for. Z3 decides bit-vector formulas mostly by bit-blasting, turning each bit into a Boolean variable and each operation into clauses over those variables (Programming Z3, §3.4.1). An AND becomes one small clause set per bit with nothing linking the bits, which fits the 2 to 6 ms that tnum_and() takes at every width, while an addition becomes a ripple-carry adder whose carries tie the bits together.

plots the cost against width. The solver's time for tnum_add() rises from 0.012 s at 8 bits to 0.77 s at 64 and its memory from 25.9 MB to 30.3 MB, by its own count. The multiplication that Linux used until 2021 is another matter, since tnum.c in 5.12 multiplied by a loop of tnum additions. Encoded with that loop unrolled once per bit, it is proved at 8 bits in 13 s. At 16 bits Z3 gives up at the 60-second limit we set and answers unknown, and the tnum paper let it run 24 hours at that width without an answer.

A wrong operation turns the answer to sat, and the model is the evidence. asks about tnum_and_broken(), which sets α to P.v and so forgets the bits that are unknown in P. Z3 picks a P with every bit unknown and a Q of all 1s, and the values x = y = 11111111.

The broken α is 0, so R claims the result is the constant 00000000 while the real x & y is 11111111. Each bit of z is one that step 2 of the proof would have placed under α. Reading a model means finding that bit and naming the step of the proof the code skipped.

A bug that shipped: the 32-bit bounds of AND, OR and XOR

Before Linux 5.7 the verifier could not follow 32-bit arithmetic and jumps closely, and programs that should have verified were refused, as the commit that changed it says. John Fastabend's commit 3f50f132d840 of 30 March 2020, "bpf: Verifier, do explicit ALU32 bounds tracking", released in 5.7, gave each register signed and unsigned bounds for its low 32 bits as well. It added one function per operator, such as scalar32_min_max_and(). Yonghong Song's 2921c90d4718 added scalar32_min_max_xor() in August 2020, released in 5.10.

The 32-bit functions for AND and OR, and then XOR, returned at once when the low halves of the source and the result were known. Their comment states the assumption: the 64-bit function, called next, would set the bounds.

The assumption was false whenever the low halves were known and the upper halves were not. Daniel Borkmann's fix, 049c4e13714e of 10 May 2021, entered 5.13-rc4 and the stable releases 5.12.4, 5.11.21 and 5.10.37, all checked against the source at those tags on git.kernel.org. The flaw is CVE-2021-3490, and the commit credits reports by Manfred Paul and by Thadeu Lima de Souza Cascardo.

Wrong bounds break the verifier's promise directly. As showed, the verifier allows an access at a pointer plus a scalar when the scalar's bounds keep it inside the object. A bound that leaves out a value the register can really hold therefore lets the check pass for that value. That is the soundness bug's cell of .

The specification the verifier needs is a claim about every input. For every pair of registers the verifier could describe before the instruction, and every pair of values inside those descriptions, the low 32 bits of the result must lie inside the 32-bit bounds the kernel computes, signed and unsigned. sets the C of scalar32_min_max_and() in v5.10 beside its encoding in book/labs/proof/cve.py, which encodes AND, OR and XOR three times each: as v5.10 had them, as the fix left them in 5.13, and as v7.2 has them. A return in the middle of the C becomes a choice at the end of the Python, because a formula has no control flow, and each C comparison has to be read for its signedness before it is written down.

A faithful encoding needs three things beyond the function itself. First, dst_known tests the destination after its tnum has been overwritten with the result of tnum_and() (line 6372 of v5.10), so it asks whether the result's low half is known. Second, scalar_min_max_and() runs next and, when both whole registers are known, calls __mark_reg_known(), which sets the 32-bit bounds as well; there the comment's assumption held. Third, the registers arrive tidied, and the encoding must say how.

An invariant is a fact about the verifier's state that every step leaves true, so the next step may rely on it. After each arithmetic instruction, __reg32_deduce_bounds() (line 1165 of v5.10) makes the signed and unsigned 32-bit ranges equal whenever either one stays on one side of the sign boundary. Then __reg_bound_offset() (line 1240) narrows the tnum to what the unsigned range allows. Without those two invariants, Z3 reports counterexamples against the fixed code as well, in states the verifier never builds. The difficulty is general: one operator cannot be judged apart from the code that tidies its output, which is the problem the work of the next chapter takes on.

gives the outcome. Z3 answers sat for all three functions of v5.10 in 0.01 to 0.03 s, and unsat for the fixed 5.13 and for v7.2.

Asked for the smallest counterexample, with bounds as tight as the tnum allows so that the numbers read easily, it returns one for AND. The destination holds 1, known in all 64 bits, and the source has its low half known 0 and bit 32 unknown. The low halves of both operands are known, so v5.10 returns early and keeps the destination's bounds of [1, 1], while the real result is 0. The source is not known in all 64 bits, so the 64-bit function repairs nothing.

The tidy-up that follows takes the larger of each pair of minima and the smaller of each pair of maxima, so it can narrow bounds but never widen them. Here it leaves the impossible state of the fixing commit's own log, a minimum of 1 above a maximum of 0. For OR, Z3 finds the mirror case, a destination of 0 and a source whose low half is 1, with bounds kept at [0, 0] and a real result of 1. The commit's own registers, a destination (0x1; 0xffffffff00000000) and the constant 0x100000002, give the same pattern, and cve.py --example evaluates them in all three kernels.

What v7.2 computes for the same registers

In v7.2 each early return calls __mark_reg32_known() (lines 14170, 14214 and 14257 of verifier.c), and the 32-bit bounds are a single circular range, r32, which introduced. scalar32_min_max_and() at line 14161 sets that arc with reg_set_urange32(), from the result tnum's value up to the smaller of the two unsigned maxima, and _or() and _xor() at lines 14205 and 14249 follow the same pattern. The v7.2 encoding needs neither of the tidy-up invariants to come out unsat, because one arc has no second range that could disagree with it.

The running kernel agrees with the formulas. book/labs/proof/v72-log.py builds a socket filter without a compiler and loads it. The filter gives R2 the value of the commit's example, (0x1; 0xffffffff00000000), ANDs it with 0x100000002 and returns 0, reading no memory, and the script prints the verifier's log for the AND. shows the result: smin32, smax32 and umax32 are 0, and the log leaves out umin32 because it is 0, the value the commit's "after" line has and the arc of cve.py --example.

Z3 proved tnum_and() in 2 to 6 ms at every width, tnum_add() in 0.012, 0.062, 0.131 and 0.771 s at 8, 16, 32 and 64 bits (medians of five) and tnum_sub() in 2.7 s at 64; the multiplication of 5.12 took 13 s at 8 bits and had no answer in 60 s at 16. The nine CVE queries took 1.4 s together, with Python's start-up.
Memory
Z3 counted at most 26.0 MB for tnum_and() and 30.3 MB for tnum_add() at 64 bits; the whole Python process peaked at 61, 67 and 77 MB for those two and the nine CVE queries. The checker stayed under 18 MB, since it holds one pair's values at a time.
Script length
tnum_add() is 11 lines of C and 4 of Python. cve.py has 168 lines of code for about 210 lines of C from two kernels: the functions under test and the tidy-up around them.

Check every case at 4 to 8 bits whenever an abstract operation changes, since it needs no tools and runs in seconds. Before posting the change, prove it with a solver at 64 and 32 bits, encoding the code that runs around it; write a proof on paper for a new algorithm whose query does not finish, as the tnum paper did for multiplication, or for a bitwise operation, where the one-bit check already is one.

When it goes wrong

Most failures in are failures of fidelity, where the formula says something other than the code. A bogus counterexample is the lucky case, because its model can be checked by hand against the C, as we did for the 32-bit bounds. The dangerous case is an unsat from a formula that says too little, assumes too much or speaks of another width, since nothing in the answer reveals it. For that reason the tnum paper ran its encodings against the kernel's C on random inputs (Appendix D). Each row's command prints its evidence within two seconds, except the timeout, which waits for the limit it is given.

Hint 1 · direction

The six operators & | ^ ~ + - work unchanged on Python integers and on Z3 bit-vectors, which is why one op() serves both checks. Keep the kernel's variable names so the code can be compared line by line.

Hint 2 · mechanism

For tnum_xor(), the value is pv ^ qv with the unknown bits cleared and the mask is pm | qm. A bit of x ^ y is unknown when it is unknown in either input.

Hint 3 · nearly there

In the model, look for a bit where Q is x. The broken mask copies P's mask only, so R claims to know that bit, and the y Z3 picked has the other value there.

Recap

Trying every case proves an abstract operation at small widths and grows sixteenfold a bit, 987 s at eight for tnum_and(). A proof on paper covers every width: with membership x & ~m == v, tnum_and() follows in six steps, each true in all 65,536 four-bit cases, and for a bitwise operation one bit suffices. A solver proves one width per query, tnum_add() at 64 bits in under a second, from the assumptions and the claim turned round, and a sat model names the bit the code got wrong. Encoded with the code around it and the verifier's invariants, the 32-bit bounds of v5.10 give a counterexample for AND, OR and XOR, the fix of 2021 and v7.2 give none, and the next chapter applies the same method to every range operation of the verifier at once.

The formula may differ from the C: an early return modelled in the wrong place, a signed comparison written with ULE, a 32-bit half compared at 64 bits, or code that runs after the function left out. Or the assumptions may be too strong, for instance an invariant the rest of the verifier does not in fact keep, or two assumptions that contradict each other, which makes every claim unsat. A third possibility is the width: a proof at 64 bits says nothing about the 32-bit path. A quick test for the second case is to drop the negated claim and check that the assumptions alone are sat; the tnum paper also ran its encodings against the C on random inputs.

v5.10 keeps [1, 1] for all four bounds, and __update_reg32_bounds() then makes them s32 [1, 0] and u32 [1, 0]; 5.13 gives [0, 0]. The result's tnum is (0x0; 0x100000000), whose low half is known 0, and R3's low half is known, so the 32-bit function returns at once, while R2 is not known in all 64 bits, so the 64-bit function does not reset the bounds. The update takes the larger minimum, 1, and the smaller maximum, the tnum's 0. cve.py --example prints these numbers. Expecting the update to repair the bounds misreads it, since it can only narrow.

That for every pair of registers whose tnums and 32-bit arcs hold their values, the arc this function computes holds every real low half of the AND: soundness of this function, as encoded, at these widths. It does not prove the 64-bit half, reg_bounds_sync() afterwards, the other operators, branches, or that the encoding matches the C; the encoding could be checked against the kernel on random inputs, as the tnum paper did. Treating one function's proof as a proof of the verifier is the overreach to avoid. The next chapter's Agni encodes every operator together with the shared tidy-up, which is how such proofs extend to the whole range analysis.

Because every p in γ(P) has at least the 1s of P.v, which is step 1 of this chapter's proof, and likewise every q, while the carry out of each position is the majority of three bits, which can only rise when an input bit rises. So setting every unknown bit to 0 carries least. Lemma 3 gives the most: Σ = (P.v + P.m) + (Q.v + Q.m), every unknown bit set to 1. A common slip is to take the mask of the sum to be P.m | Q.m alone, which is the broken tnum_add_nocarry of the refresher's checker; the carries that differ between the two extremes must be added.

Bit 1 is unknown in both inputs, so bit 1 of the sum may be 0 + 0, which carries nothing, or 1 + 1, which carries into bit 2; the minimum carries 10000 and the maximum 10100 differ there. The proof of tnum_add() must therefore follow carries across bits, and Lemma 5 shows that the kernel's mask (sv ⊕ Σ) | P.m | Q.m equals the one built from the carries. tnum_and() computes each bit of the result from the same bit of its inputs alone, so there are no carries to track. It is tempting to think the unknown carry comes from bit 0, but 0 + 1 there carries nothing.

What this shows. In the broken run every bit of Q is unknown, yet R claims to know all eight bits of x ^ y; Z3 chose y = 11111111, which flips every bit, so z = 00000000 disagrees with R everywhere. The dropped | qm would have marked those bits unknown. For subtraction the dropped term is the borrows, the counterpart of the carries in the reading above.

Common wrong turn. Writing concrete() as something other than the instruction's effect, such as x | y for XOR, which makes the real operation look broken. Another is copying tnum_sub() with a.mask and b.mask swapped in alpha and beta, which the checker catches at once.
