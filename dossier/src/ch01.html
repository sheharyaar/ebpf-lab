Two machines, twenty-one years apart

Look at the left side of the two-machines figure (figure 1.1). That is the 1993 machine: the accumulator A, an index register X, a few words of scratch memory, and one packet in. Linux ran it for years for tcpdump's socket filters, and later for seccomp's system-call filters.

Still runningThe left column has not gone away. Run tcpdump -d 'tcp port 22': it prints the classic program tcpdump compiles. Linux rewrites such filters into the new instructions before it runs them.

In 2014 Alexei Starovoitov rebuilt that machine inside the kernel as the one on the right. LWN called it "internal BPF" at the time; the kernel documentation now calls it eBPF, extended BPF. Linux 3.15 shipped it in June 2014 and converted every classic filter into the new instructions, so tcpdump never noticed.

Read the right column top to bottom. Each row answers a limit of the left one. Ten 64-bit registers, r0 to r9, match the registers of a modern 64-bit CPU.

A read-only frame pointer, r10, points at a small stack. A call instruction reaches into the kernel. Linux 3.18 added maps in December 2014, so a program could keep state between runs.

"The latter expands the set of available registers from two to ten, adds a number of instructions that closely match real hardware instructions, implements 64-bit registers, makes it possible for BPF programs to call a (rigidly controlled) set of kernel functions, and more."

Jonathan Corbet, "BPF: the universal in-kernel virtual machine", LWN, 21 May 2014

Eight bytes per instruction

Every eBPF instruction is eight bytes, cut into the fields of the encoding figure (figure 1.2). The first byte is the opcode: what to do, such as move, add, jump or call. The next byte holds two register numbers of four bits each, a source and a destination. Then come a signed 16-bit offset, used for memory addresses and jump distances, and a signed 32-bit immediate, a constant carried inside the instruction.

The two lower rows are your own program. Slot 4 is bf a2: a 64-bit move whose source is r10 and whose destination is r2. Slot 8 is 85, the call opcode, with 1 in its immediate. Your machine is little-endian, so the bytes 01 00 00 00 are the number 1. Together these instructions are your program's bytecode: what clang -target bpf writes into the object file, and what the kernel receives.

One kind of instruction does not fit in eight bytes. Loading a full 64-bit constant needs a wide instruction, which takes two slots and sixteen bytes. Your program has three of them. That is why the disassembler's slot numbers jump from 6 to 8.

Calling into the kernel

Your program calls into the kernel four times; the bytecode figure (figure 1.3) highlights the first two. Each target is a helper function: a function inside the kernel that a program may call by number. That number is the helper ID, and it sits in the immediate. Helper 14 returns the current process and thread IDs, and helper 1 looks up a key in a map. The full list is the BPF_FUNC_ enum in the vmlinux.h your program already includes.

Arguments reach a helper through a fixed calling convention, stepped through in the helper-call figure (figure 1.4). Registers r1 to r5 carry up to five arguments, and r0 carries the answer back. After the call, r1 to r5 cannot be read; the kernel documentation says they are "reset to unreadable". Registers r6 to r9 keep their values across the call, and r10 never changes.

Your slot 1Slot 1 stores w0, the low 32 bits of r0; every register has a w name for its low half. Helper 14 puts the thread ID in that low half and the process ID in the high half, so your table counts writes per thread. In a single-threaded process the two numbers are the same.

Watch r1 in your listing. The map goes into r1 at slot 6 and again at slot 21, because no value in r1 is kept across a call. A value the program needs after a call has to be parked in r6 to r9.

Five arguments is a deliberate limit. A 64-bit CPU passes a function's first arguments in registers, and r1 to r5 can sit in exactly those registers. On x86-64 the kernel documentation pairs r1 with rdi and r0 with rax, the registers an ordinary kernel function already uses. A call from a program then needs no registers shuffled before it.

Recap

The 2014 machine keeps the 1993 shape and widens it: ten 64-bit registers, a read-only frame pointer over a stack, calls into the kernel, and maps. Every instruction is eight bytes, cut into opcode, two register numbers, offset and immediate; a 64-bit constant takes a second slot. A helper call carries the helper's ID in the immediate, its arguments in r1 to r5 and its answer in r0, and it leaves r1 to r5 unreadable.

1.1

24 instructions in 216 bytes. Every slot is eight bytes, so the section holds 27 × 8 = 216 bytes. Three of the slots are the second halves of wide instructions, so there are 27 − 3 = 24 instructions. Check it with llvm-readelf -S /tmp/hashmap.bpf.o: the section named after your hook has size 0xd8, which is 216. The near-miss is 27 instructions: the disassembler numbers slots, which is why the line after the wide instruction at slot 6 is slot 8.

1.2

The program read r1 after a call, and after a call r1 holds nothing readable. A helper call leaves r1 to r5 unreadable whatever the helper did, so after call 1 the map is no longer in r1. Load the map into r1 again before the next call, as your own slot 21 does, or keep a value you need in r6 to r9. The rule is in the kernel's "Classic BPF vs eBPF" page: after a call, "R1 - R5 are reset to unreadable". The near-miss is to think the helper overwrote r1 with junk; the kernel never looks at what the helper did, it applies the rule to every call.

1.3

No. The zero is a placeholder. The line under it, R_BPF_64_64 write_counts, is a relocation: a note telling the loader that this instruction must point at the map named write_counts. The map does not exist yet when clang writes the file. When your loader creates it, libbpf writes the map's file descriptor into the instruction and marks it as a map reference, and the kernel swaps in the map itself; section 5.4 of the RFC lists that form. The load-and-attach chapter walks through it. The near-miss is to read 0x0 as NULL; nothing runs from the file as written.

1.4

The jump class, and a call that takes its target from the immediate. The low three bits of 0x85 are 101, which is 0x5, the JMP class in the class table of section 3.3. The top four bits are 1000, which is 0x8, CALL in the jump table of section 4.3. The bit between them is 0, which section 4 calls K: the operand is the immediate. The near-miss is to look for CALL among the arithmetic instructions; CALL and EXIT both live in the jump class.

1.5

Because the immediate holds only 32 bits. A 64-bit constant, such as an address, cannot fit in one slot, so the wide encoding of section 3.2 appends a second eight bytes whose immediate carries the upper half. Section 5.4 gives the arithmetic: dst = (next_imm << 32) | imm. Your object uses it three times, twice for the map and once for the bpf_printk format string. The near-miss is to think the wide form exists for long jumps; in this standard only the 64-bit immediate loads use it.

1.6

An ID, and nothing else about the helper. Section 4.3.1 says a helper was identified by a static ID in the immediate, and that platforms with kernel type information may put a type ID there instead. What each helper does, which arguments it takes and what it returns are left to the platform: on Linux, the kernel's helper documentation and the BPF_FUNC_ list. That is why helper 1 means "map lookup" on Linux without the standard saying so. The near-miss is to expect the RFC to list helpers; it leaves that to future work.

Lab 1.1

$ # from the playground root
$ clang -g -O2 -target bpf -D__TARGET_ARCH_x86 -I/usr/include/bpf -I. \
    -c maps/02-hash-map/hashmap.bpf.c -o /tmp/hashmap.bpf.o
$ llvm-objdump -dr /tmp/hashmap.bpf.o | grep 'call 0x'
       0:	85 00 00 00 0e 00 00 00	call 0xe
       8:	85 00 00 00 01 00 00 00	call 0x1
      16:	85 00 00 00 06 00 00 00	call 0x6
      24:	85 00 00 00 02 00 00 00	call 0x2
$ grep -nE 'BPF_FUNC_[a-z_]+ = (1|2|6|14),' vmlinux.h
13320:	BPF_FUNC_map_lookup_elem = 1,
13321:	BPF_FUNC_map_update_elem = 2,
13325:	BPF_FUNC_trace_printk = 6,
13333:	BPF_FUNC_get_current_pid_tgid = 14,

Expected output. The four calls above, in that order; clang also prints a few warnings from vmlinux.h, which are harmless. Your line numbers in vmlinux.h depend on the kernel it was generated from. Run on kernel 7.2 with clang 23, November 2026.

What this shows. call 0xe is helper 14, bpf_get_current_pid_tgid; call 0x1 is the map lookup; call 0x6 is bpf_trace_printk, which is what bpf_printk becomes; call 0x2 is the map update. At slot 8, r1 holds the map loaded at slot 6, and r2 holds r10 − 4, the address of the key on the stack.

Common wrong turn. Reading 0xe as decimal, or as an address. It is hexadecimal 14, a helper ID. The other is looking for bpf_printk in the list: it is a macro over helper 6.
